Length normalisation is not a new problem. The probabilistic relevance framework already accounts for document length when scoring, and does so precisely because longer documents accumulate term matches without becoming more relevant (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009). What has not been settled is the same question one level down, where the unit being scored is a chunk the system itself chose.

We define recall at rank k over a query set Q in the usual way, so that our numbers can be read against the retrieval literature without translation:

R@k  =  1|Q|  ∑q∈Q  1[ rank(q) ≤ k ]
(3)

The effect survives when overlap is held at zero, which rules out the simplest explanation — that longer chunks merely absorb the boundaries that shorter ones straddle.

Retrieval-augmented generation established the retrieve-then-generate pattern that most current systems still follow (Lewis et al., 2020), and the dense retrieval work it builds on fixed passages at roughly one hundred words (Karpukhin et al., 2020). That choice has been inherited far more often than it has been re-examined.

Two lines of work complicate it. Late interaction defers the comparison to the token level, which changes what a badly placed boundary costs (Khattab & Zaharia, 2020). Fusion-in-decoder scales the number of passages rather than their size, and reports gains from doing so (Izacard & Grave, 2021).

Neither evaluates at book length, and that is the gap this paper occupies — a narrower gap than our first draft claimed.
