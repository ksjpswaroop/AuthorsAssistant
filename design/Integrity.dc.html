4. Nightfall at the Rim

The rim of the basin held the last of the heat the way a kiln holds it, grudgingly and all at once. Tamsin counted forty paces out to the marker stone and stopped counting when the wind erased her fortieth footprint before she had finished making it.

Below them the salt went on being white in the dark, refusing to be black like everything else1 — she wanted to be exact about that later, when the cartographer asked her to write it down.

“We cross at moonrise or we do not cross,” he said. He had said it four times since noon, each time as though the thought had only that moment arrived and surprised him.

Evaporite basins of this kind form where inflow is exceeded by evaporation over geological timescales, leaving a crust of halite several metres deep2. Tamsin had read that somewhere and repeated it to herself like a prayer she did not believe.

The vast expanse of the salt flats stretched endlessly before them, a testament to the relentless power of nature and the passage of time. It was a moment that would stay with her forever, a reminder of both the beauty and the danger of the world she had chosen.3

When the moon came up it came up wrong — too far south, too thin, the colour of a coin left out in weather. She checked the marker stone. She checked it again.
